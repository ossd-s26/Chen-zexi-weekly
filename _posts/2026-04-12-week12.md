## Why Companies Care About Open Source

This week's discussion on business models around open source challenged something I used to assume: that companies use open source because it's free. Turns out the real reasons are more strategic. Avoiding vendor lock-in, attracting talent, accelerating innovation through shared tooling, and ensuring interoperability. Cost savings are almost a side effect.

A key idea from the lecture: **open source is not a business model**. It's a distribution strategy that businesses build revenue models *around*. That's why so many different models coexist, from Red Hat's subscriptions to GitLab's open core to MongoDB's dual licensing.

## Red Hat and vLLM

I actually first encountered Red Hat not through Linux, but through [vLLM](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm), one of the most widely used inference engines in LLM infrastructure. vLLM started as a research project out of UC Berkeley's Sky Lab, and companies like NVIDIA and AMD actively contribute to it. When I was browsing the contributor list, I noticed Red Hat engineers were consistently among the top contributors. That confused me. On paper, Red Hat has nothing to do with an LLM inference engine. They're not an AI company, they don't sell GPU hardware. So why are their engineers putting serious effort into this project?

That question is what made this week's discussion click. Red Hat's whole business is about taking open source software and providing the enterprise support, certification, and stability that large organizations need to run it in production. The more critical open source infrastructure exists, the more demand there is for someone to make it enterprise-ready. Contributing to vLLM isn't charity. It's investing in future revenue. Every company that adopts vLLM at scale is a potential Red Hat customer.

## The Same Pattern Everywhere

Once I saw this with Red Hat, I started recognizing the same thing across the modern stack. **PostgreSQL** has been open source for decades, and companies like **Supabase** build managed platforms on top of it. Supabase gives away the database (it was never theirs to sell) and charges for the hosted service, auth layer, and real-time features.

It's even more visible in the LLM application space I'm familiar with. **LangChain** open-sourced their framework and it blew up almost overnight. But the business isn't the framework. It's **LangSmith**, their paid observability and evaluation platform for running LLM apps at scale. Open source gets you adoption, then you capture revenue from businesses that need operational tooling to use it seriously.

The insight across all these cases: open source doesn't just make software free. It can be a real business if you're the one helping industry use it at scale, long-term, with reliability guarantees.
